Russellian semantics

short

The view, at least intermittently held by Russell, that propositions are composed of the “objects, properties and relations” that they represent.

main

According to the Stanford Encyclopedia entry Theories of Meaning Russellian semantics is the doctrine that the constituents of propositions (i.e. sentence meanings) are “objects, properties, relations and functions”.

Russellian semantics is basically Fregean_semantics (Frege's distinction between sense and reference) with the added claim that senses (at least the fundamental ones) are (the same as) the properties(/relationships) of the objects that they represent. (Though in these cases the distinction between sense and reference may collapse.)

According to Russell:

when there is an anything with which we do not have immediate acquaintance, but only definition by denoting phrases, then the propositions in which this thing is introduced by means of a denoting phrase do not really contain this thing as a constituent, but contain instead the constituents expressed by the several words of the denoting phrase. Thus in every proposition that we can apprehend (i.e. not only in those whose truth or falsehood we can judge of, but in all that we can think about), all the constituents are really entities with which we have immediate acquaintance.
russell1905-onDenoting.pdf

The objects and properties that are directly named by genuine names and predicates are sensibilia (sense data - but they can exist independently of any mind) and their properties. (These sensibilia are the basic elements of Russell's metaphysics and ordinary physical objects are “logical constructions” from them). But note that we do not have any names for individual sensibilia and hence it seems there are no genuine names at all. Russell's account of these semantic foundations works best when applied to predicates. We are directly acquainted with the property red, and it is impossible for us to form the judgement that we are seeing a red sensible without there being a red sensible. Our having the thought guarantees the existence of the property.

Still note the similarity to the traditional Aristotelian account of representation – at least within the fundamental sphere to which we have direct epistemic (and semantic) access. (see wn.RLN:collapse_of_traditional_European_accounts_of_reasoning# second problem: the content of scientific theories )

Russellian-Millian semantics

The Millian addition is that the meaning of a “simple proper name” is the object it refers to. Odd since, at least on its most obvious reading Russell's account does away with simple proper names altogether (unless one takes predicates to be names of properties, but even then predicates are not simple proper names).

descriptivism

russell1905-onDenoting.pdf It is odd that the chief objection to “Russellian-Millian” semantics raised in the Stanford Encyclopedia entry is the problem of informative identities, since Russell's most celebrated article (largely responsible for establishing his early reputation as an first rate philosopher) was devoted to proposing a solution.

problem

informative identities

If the meaning of a name is the object it denotes (i.e. two names are synonymous if they denote the same object) then the principle that substituting one synonym for another preserves the meaning of the overall sentence requires that the following two sentences are synonyms:

  1. Clark Kent is Clark Kent.
  2. Clark Kent is Superman.

So too the following two sentences should be synonymous, but they differ in meaning so significantly that the first is true and the second false:

  1. Lois believes that Clark Kent is Clark Kent.
  2. Lois believes that Clark Kent is Superman.

The usual reason given for believing that (1) and (2) differ in meaning is their means of justification. (1) is justified by the a priori insight that each thing is identical to itself, (2) by a posteriori investigation. The difference in truth value between (3) and (4) is enough to establish that they are different in meaning (and hence that the sentential clause versions of (1) and (2) that they contain must differ in meaning at least in this context). (see truth_conditional_semantics#objections#propositional attitudes)

meaningful negative existentials

If the meaning of a name is the object it denotes, then what about the meaning of denials of existence such as “Prester John does not exist”. If Prester John does not exist then how can a meaningful sentence contain a meaningless name? But if Prester John does exist, then the denial is false. So no denial of existence can ever be true! (A problem raised as far back as Parmenides' poem On Nature.) Nor indeed any meaningful statements about non-existent objects, such as “The King of France is bald.”

What then are the meanings of names if not the object they denote?

solution

Russell's solution to this “Millian problem” was to claim that “Superman” (at least, but “Clark Kent” also) is not a “simple proper name” but a (hidden) definite description. The name “Superman” is synonymous with a definite description (say “the one and only humanoid born on Krypton possessing superhuman strength, the power of flight, superhuman vision and hearing, spends much of his time of Earth, often wears red and blue” or some more extended version including all the information about Superman that is provided to us in the canonical media).

Thus (2) is synonymous with:

2') Clark Kent is the one and only humanoid born on Krypton possessing superhuman strength …

Now “Clark Kent” is presumably also a definite description. Perhaps something like “the one and only tall male who works as a reporter at the Daily Planet, wears glasses etc.,”. In any case (1) with or without “Clark Kent” replaced by its synonymous definite description is clearly different in meaning from (2').

So too it may well be true that:

3') Lois believes that the one and only tall male who works as a reporter at the Daily Planet and wears glasses is the one and only tall male who works as a reporter …

and yet false that:

4') Lois believes that the one and only tall male who works as a reporter at the Daily Planet and wear glasses is the one and only humanoid born on Krypton …

It is also straightforwardly false that:

One and only one person is King of France and that person is bald.

Hence the problem posed by the informativeness of some identity statements and the meaningfulness of negative existentials is resolved if these names are really definite descriptions.

denoting phrases meaningless in isolation

Above Russell's solution was described as hinging on the claim that (some) names are hidden definite descriptions. Which is to say that names are synonymous with definite descriptions. (e.g. that “Superman” is synonymous with “the one and only humanoid from Krypton …”). But according to Russell these denoting phrases (including definite descriptions) have no meaning at all.

According to the view which I advocate, a denoting phrase is essentially part of a sentence, and does not, like most single words, have any significance on its own account.

WHY? To accept that “the meanings of words are abstractions from the truth conditions of the sentences that contain them”†Quine, Five milestones of empiricism in quine1981-theoriesAndThings.pdf is not to deny that they have meanings at all. If substituting one phrase for another always produces a sentence synonymous with the original, then we may well say that the two phrases are themselves synonymous. And if “Superman” is synonymous (in this sense) with “the one and only humanoid born on Krypton …” (as well as a range of other phrases) we can (if there are no better candidates) identify this class of synonyms as their common meaning.

Indeed, in the quote above Russell explicitly states that “most single words” have “significance on [their] own account”. So Russell is not claiming that sentences are the only bearers of meaning and none of the constituent parts have meaning by themselves. What then is the reason for claiming that these denoting phrases have no meaning in isolation?

The phrase per se has no meaning, because in any proposition in which it occurs the proposition, fully expressed, does not contain the phrase, which has been broken up.

This is implausible. Russell (as well as nearly everyone else) thinks that statements have meanings (i.e. propositions). Statements/propositions are complex “logical constructions” from meaningful basic elements (predicates which express directly known properties). Like predicates(/properties) that have meaning in isolation definite descriptions contribute to the meaning of the statements(/propositions) that contain them. So there appears to be no reason not to admit definite descriptions as meaningful, though admittedly incomplete, logical constructions from the same materials.

Wittgenstein's solution

There is however, an ulterior motive lurking nearby. Russell's self-proclaimed objective in Principia Mathematica was to reduce mathematics to (Fregean first order) logic. In this the number one was identified with the set of all the sets that contain one object. (The set of all the sets S such that ∃x∀y x∊S ∧.y∊S ⊃ y=x.) This set was then identified with the propositional function “… is a set such that ∃x∀y x∊S ∧.y∊S ⊃ y=x”. And then that propositional function (the complex predicate involved in a definite description) could be dispensed with. Leaving us with a mathematics that carries no ontological commitments at all, and hence the reduction to first order logic (which is supposed to carry no ontological commitments) was complete. (this ulterior motive confirmed in soames2007-noClassRussellOnContextualDefinitionAndTheEliminationOfSets.pdf)

objections to other aspects of Russell's semantics

I seem to remember an issue along the lines: if true propositions are facts then what are the false propositions? If the proposition that a statement expresses is “objects properties and relations” in a particular structure/arrangement/state of affairs then what about false statements. If there is no such arrangement then is the statement meaningless? If there is then is the statement true?

This is one of the “museum theories” of language to which Quine so opposed. The indeterminacy of translation is the key objection to sentence meanings.
