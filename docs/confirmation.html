main

When one conducts an experiment to test a hypothesis. There is a division between the rules of the theory that are taken to be, at least provisionally, necessary. (That was a misguided and misleading way of putting it). We, at least provisionally, take some part of the theory as beyond question. If things go badly and in attempting to determine whether of hypothesis is true (e.g. the value of a particular quantity or constant has no solution - i.e. no value will satisfy the constraints of our theory and our observations) then we may cast doubt on the theory itself, but if things go well we simply rely on the theory to compute the truth value(/quantitative value) In the extreme (and paradigmatic case) they are taken to be nomologically necessary (and hence, at least, true at all times and places#*1) but they may simply be invariant between experiment instances.

*1

That this is not so much as assumption of the regularity of nature, as an attempt to discern whatever it is that is regular/invariant. We do not optimistically assume that the laws we discover are invariant, but look for whatever it is that is invariant and call them laws.

It is these invariant, nomologically necessary (which is to say regarded or held to be nomologically necessary principles) which constitute the relevant “scientific theory”. Newton's theory of gravitation, or the theory of quantum mechanics, or general relativity are theories in this sense. Consisting purely of principles that are taken to be true at all times and places. In general this is what is meant by a scientific theory. And when we talk of a scientific theory being confirmed it is such theories that provide the paradigmatic examples.

But, of course, there are scientific theories about particulars too. The big bang theory or the theory that the Vinland map is a forgery, or the theory that humans first non-seasonal settlements predate the development of agriculture.

These are at best second rate theories. They are apt to be called hypotheses rather than theories, or once strongly confirmed called discoveries rather than theories. They are applications of the invariant theory, plus particular observations to determine other particulars. In the worst case they may lead to revision of a proper invariant theory when no hypotheses regarding the further particulars can explain/fit with the observed evidence.

ad hoc hypotheses

my_thesis problem Ad hoc means "for this" and refers to something created or done for a specific purpose or problem, often on a temporary basis.

An Ad hoc hypothesis allows one to maintain a theory without modification in the face of conflicting evidence. So theory T predicts that we will observe O. We don't observe O but we don't change our confidence in T or our expectations of the future observations it predicts.

Now we might have an explanation of why the predictions didn't come off in this case that are not ad hoc. Perhaps the switch was off, or a fuse had burnt out. But then we can investigate and make observations to independently confirm this extraneous factor. And we will lose credence in our theory if that factor doesn't occur in future and the prediction still don't come off. The ad hoc assumption becomes an enthymeme in hindsight.

is it confirmation if we dont know it yet?

confirmation_holism RLN Suppose that someone hasn't worked out that observation or other is a deductive consequence of a theory.

We don't even need to engage the machinery of hypothetico-deductivism to describe such a situation. Suppose there is some observable thing that, once it has been worked out, is widely taken as very strong evidence for believing the theory in question, but was known for some time before the connection was worked out.

One supposes that something like this must have occurred in the development of general relativity. Certainly, that general relativity explained (/retrodicted) the advance in mercury's perehelion was given by Einstein as a reason to believe general relativity and for many people this may be one of the first things they are told about general relativity, so that there is no period of time in which they understand the theory but do not know that it has this consequence. But, at least for Einstein himself, surely the situation was otherwise. There must, of course, have been a period in which he suspected, but was not absolutely sure that general relativity explained this phenomena. He must have been motivated to try to explain Mercury's perehelion, prior to actually coming up with the explanation. But even before this - surely there was a time when he was aware of the theory of relativity but had not yet connected it with Mercury's perehelion. Surely we have good (though not conclusive) reason for beleiving that there was a time after Einstein first formulated the theory of general relativity, but before he considered whether there was a connection between the two. Actually no explaiing the advance perehelion may have been one of his initial reasons for developing general relativity in the first place but it doesnt matter - the mere possibility is enough, though difficult for a modal skeptic to build upon

In the other direction we say that XYZ observation confirmed belief in a theory. Say the observation of the deflection of starlight around the Sun in 1919. Until the observation ws made and it caused people confidence in T to increase.

objection: it ought to be categorical

If one provides “one ought to reaons well if one wants to manifest the structure of their beliefs in words” of “if they want to maintain a unchanging connection between their linguistic expressions and their non-linguistic models” then this is wrong.

One ought to reason well FULL STOP One does not reason well to achieve this or that objective. And the person who reasons badly is doing something wrong or incorrect – not merely something that does not lead to outcomes that they desire.

This is silly - we do not know why we do it, what goal it fulfilled. But this isn't to say that there is one. We can in hindsight say that it was, in some sense, assumed that everyone should maintain a consistent mapping between linguistic and non-linguistic representations, or perhaps that everyone should be able to explain themselves and their motivations (consider the teachers anger when a student says they don't know why they misbehaved). But this was simply an ignorant and mistaken theory. There was some benefit in doing so, but one that (ironically enough) we could not articulate and so we simply said God requires it, or in some other way it is simply virtuous to do this.

We are disagreeing with the holders of those views. We have a different account of what reasoning is . But that difference alone does not guarantee that they are right and we are wrong. We ought not, at least if we are to reason well, dismiss all non-normative accounts of reasoning simply because the ancients though reasoning well was an end in itself.

One may as well say, it is an essential part of reasoning that it is mysterious why we should reason well. Therefore no unmysterious explanation of reasoning can ever be correct
