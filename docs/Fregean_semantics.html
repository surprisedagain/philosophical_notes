main

Frege maintained that there are things that stand in much the same relationship to (n-place) predicates (and sentences) that name stand in to objects. In each case there is the symbol, the sense that it expresses and the object that it denotes.

Statements express intentions and denote the truth values true and false.

Just as names have sense and reference (the mode of presentation which singles out the object and the object denoted) sentences have a sense (an intension which is, or at least fixes, its truth conditions) and denote a truth value (true or false - somewhat akin to the way definite descriptions denote an object, but which object depends on how the world is)

Note that just as substituting extensionally equivalent predicates (or descriptions) does not alter the truth value of extensional statements. So too substituting one sentential clause for another naming the same truth value will not change the truth value of a truth functional statement.

Fregean senses

Senses are both objective because they can be shared - you and I use the term “fire-engine” with the same sense (among other things it is true of the same objects) regardless of our personal ideas about fire engines.

But they are subjective in that they are the “modes of presentation” by which external objects (and properties) are presented to the mind.

To at least a first approximation, Fregean senses are concepts.

In the case of names, this seems to amount to taking names to be hidden definite descriptions. But I would like to see an example of this attitude being taken to predicates (and the properties that their senses presumably present to the mind).

Freges criterion of synonymy:

Two sentences S and S* differ in sense if and only if some rational agent who understood both could, on reflection, judge that S is true without judging that S* is true.
(A Brief Survey of my Logical Doctrines, 1906)

objections

all necessary truths synonymous

An immediate consequence of Frege's criterion of synonymy is that all necessary truths are synonymous (this is true of all forms of truth functional semantics; see truth_conditional_semantics#objections#necessary truths ).

But Frege does seem to have a way out here. Even if two statements have the same truth conditions they may achieve those conditions by combining different senses. Meanings are structures that fix but are not the same as truth conditions. This means giving up the criteria above and replacing it with the following: 

	S and S* differ in sense if some rational agent who understood both could
	judge that S is true without judging that S* is true.
    AND
    Expressions E and E* differ in sense iff there is some sentence S which
    contains E, and substituting E* for E in S produces a statement that a
    rational agent could judge true without judging that S is true
    AND
	S and S* differ in sense iff they have different logical/intensional
	structures or contain expressions with different senses in some position in
	their logical/intensional structure.

Kripkes modal argument

against names as definite descriptions Frege can appeal to the sense of names to explain how there are informative identity statements. (see Russellian_semantics#informative identity statements )

But treating names as disguised definite descriptions seems to give rise to further problems: from: kripke1980-namingAndNecessity#lecture 1

  1. Necessarily, if Aristotle exists, then Aristotle is Aristotle.
  2. Necessarily, if Aristotle exists, then Aristotle is the greatest philosopher of antiquity.

If for each (meaningful) name there is a definite description (such as “greatest philosopher of antiquity”) expressing the same sense (at least for one subject or other) then replacing the name with that definite description should leave the meaning of the sentence unchanged. However, replacing the last “Aristotle” in (1) with the matching definite description produces (2) which seems to have a different truth value. It seems that no matter what definite description we put in its place the same problem will arise.

However, Frege can maintain that “In the case of genuinely proper names like 'Aristotle' opinions as regards their sense may diverge.” But, “As long as the nominatum remains the same, these fluctuations in sense are tolerable”.

Although this only frees Frege from claiming that there is some shared sense of the name he still appears to have to maintain that there is some such definite description/sense for each person who can use that name correctly.

However, perhaps there is a sufficiently complex definite description that will do the job. Consider if Mary claims that Jesus didn't live in Palestine, didn't live during the 1st century CE, wasn't Jewish, wasn't male, didn't preach about compassion or service to others and was not crucified. Perhaps she believes that Jesus is the person who lives at the end of her street. Perhaps she maintains that the person who lives at the end of her street is the messiah, the Son of God and the Son of Man; or that in some other way the man living at the end of her street possesses some of the properties (the most important ones) attributed to Jesus in the canonical accounts of him. But maybe she doesn't believe that anyone even approximately fulfills the description of Jesus given in the gospels and yet that the person at the end of her street is Jesus.

It seems like a natural reaction is to ask “In what sense are they Jesus?” It does seem that there may be some truly complex definite description, some sort of weighted cluster of properties and relations such that if an object doesn't fulfill them then it is not possible for them to be Jesus (mutatis mutandis Aristotle). This seems like it could be combined with lewis1972-psychophysicalAndTheoreticalIdentifications – It is not possible for an object that does not come remotely near enough to fulfilling our theory of Jesus to be Jesus.

If we combine this with the variability of the sense from one speaker to another it seems like we can get quite a reasonable defence going. The objection is now that no-one can give this complex definite description on request.

other defences: Plantinga, 1978, The Boethian Compromise Dummett, 1981, The Interpretation of Frege’s Philosophy Sosa, 2001, Rigidity in the Scope of Russell’s Theory counter: Soames, 1998, The Modal Argument: Wide Scope and Rigidified Descriptions 2002, Beyond Rigidity: The Unfinished Semantic Agenda of Naming and Necessity Caplan, 2005, Against Widescopism a further defence: Rothschild, 2007, Presuppositions and Scope

unknown synonyms

from Salmon 1990 “A Millian Heir Rejects the Wages of Sinn”, in Propositional Attitudes: The Role of Content in Logic, Language, and Mind

  1. Bob believes that catsup is catsup.
  2. Bob believes that catsup is ketchup.

The objection goes: (3) is true and (4) is false (by hypothesis Bob falsely believes that these are two different sauces). So it appears that Frege must deny that “catsup” and “ketchup” are synonymous. This suggests that terms(mass terms/predicates) are only synonymous if it is impossible to believe just one of them. It seems that Frege must multiply senses to an implausible degree. (In brief: if “catsup” and “ketchup” aren't synonyms then nothing is.)

response

Bob is using language improperly, or at least idiosyncratically. He doesn't have the concept “catsup” (or even “ketchup”) – which is to say he has no term that expresses the (identical) senses of those terms. Hence nether (7) nor (8) is true. Bob may believe that the condiment commonly called “catsup” is commonly called “catsup” and (erroneously) that the condiment commonly called “catsup” is not commonly called “ketchup”, but he lacks the concepts.

Bob is still of course an English speaker, and hence what he is doing is wrong. But his (norm breaching) error is to have matched up “catsup” with the wrong concept. He is making a (linguistic) error – but he still doesn't believe either (3) or (4).
