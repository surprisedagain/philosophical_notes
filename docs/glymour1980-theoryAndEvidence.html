chapter 5

positive account begins computations and representations

equation version

A theory is a set of equations.

A computation is a tree in which the nodes are quantities (variables contained in the theories equations, e.g. constants whose value is determined by experiment, or quantitative properties of the objects that the theory concerns) whose values can be determined from the values satisfying their child nodes and the equations of the theory.

zero-level nodes are experimentally determined quantities. (E.g. the weight, color, length or position of an ordinary middle sized object.)

It seems that any computation tree can be flattened into just two levels (zero and first) with all the zero-level nodes on which the computation is based retained, and one first-level node for each quantity (variable) to which the theory assigns a value. (Which can be deduced from the zero-level values by use of the theory equations.)

So we cannot in general speak of a given quantity as being of a certain level (always being represented by a node with the same level in every computation). Nor even say that a particular quantity (variable) is of a higher level than another (unless every computation that fixes the former also fixes the latter). and, as might be expected, Glymour goes on to do exactly this on page 124

So we must regard having a multi-level computation (as in the given examples) as a common practice rather than a requirement. Scientists often rely upon theory and evidence to deduce the value of particular quantities that objects in that theory are related to. The gravitational constant, the mass of any ordinary object (even one whose mass we cant directly or easily determine). They then use that quantity in combination with further evidence to deduce predictions of future observations. Determining (the numerical value of) a single property often only involves a fragment of a particular theory.

If we did take having a multi-level tree as a requirement then it would mean that each quantity would have to be deducible (perhaps indirectly) from theory and the quantities of zero-level nodes. (However, this is also true of the flattened tree, the representation.) And in each step of the method by which it is derived a value of a quantity is newly determined. This would seem to preclude a simultaenous equation with several solutions. That might be the result of some experiment, and then some later experiment might either add another constraint, or somehow eliminate some (all but one) of those solutions.

the bootstrapping condition

This is the technical heart of Glymour's positive account

first the equation version:

For a set of values of quantities to confirm an equation with respect to a theory consisting of a set of equations, then, all of the following should obtain: the set of values should be consistent with the theory; from the values for the initial quantities, a value for each quantity in the equation tested should be computable; the computation should involve only equations in the theory or their consequences; the equation that represents the hypothesis tested for the set of computations used should not be a mathematical identity; and the values obtained from the computations should constitute a solution to the equation that is tested.

The (non-technical summary of) full first order logic version: (The tie to Hempel's satisfaction condition is supposed to be replaceable.)

Let E and H be sentences, T a consistent, deductively closed collection of sentences. E confirms H with respect to T if:

  1. T is consistent with H & E.
  2. The second condition says, in effect, that every quantity (open atomic sentence) occurring in the hypothesis can be computed from the evidence, using the theory.
  3. Hempel's satisfaction condition
  4. is little more than the requirement that the computations not guarantee that the hypothesis will be confirmed (or at least not disconfirmed), whatever the evidence might say.
  5. is needed in order to ensure that the computations do not guarantee part of what the hypothesis asserts, only testing a remainder.

relevance

(scientific anti-holism) good-point Glymour's account does seem to give a good sense of why scientists may feel holism is at odds with their normal practice. I don't “test the whole theory” when I determine the mass of my car, nor even of Mars. I use some of the theory to determine the value of some qualities and when they give me inconsistent values or values that do not satisfy the theoretical constraints then they cast doubt on the part of the theory used to compute(/deduce) those values from the observed quantities. If the values computed satisfy the hypothesis then the evidence upon which that computation was based confirm that hypothesis (plus, one imagines [though Perhaps Glymour does not] the part of the theory used to compute those values); and if they do not satisfy the hypothesis then the evidence upon which the computation was based infirm it (again plus the fragment of supporting theory, though the drop in credence need not be the same).

The particular example to which Glymour repeatedly returns is Kepler's three laws of planetary motion.

To test Kepler’s third law we need estimates of the periods and mean distances from the sun of at least two planets, but from the observations of one planet alone we cannot compute, using Kepler’s laws and their consequences, the parameters of the orbit of any other planet.

So certainly if Kepler's third law hadn't been verified there would be a strong appetite for doing so, and doing so would increase our confidence in Kepler's third law (seemingly reasonably enough).
