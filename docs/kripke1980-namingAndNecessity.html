rigid designation akin to scope

The whole rigid designator issue is a kind of scope error.

I = the person who utters this statement = ∃x x utters this statement . . .

Of course the “this” is still indexical. It may be that the ancients were wrong and indexicals are ineliminable. But the relationship that holds between the indexical term (token) itself and the object it refers to can be. If we think of the scope of that relationship as being inside or outside the scope of the quantifiers in the (analysed) statement itself. Rigid is tight scope, non-rigid is wide scope.

The model theory made this completely clear, though it should have been clear enough on the intuitive level. Waiving fussy considerations deriving from the fact that x need not have necessary existence, it was clear from (x) ☐(x = x) and Leibnitz's law that identity is an 'internal' relation: (x) (y) (x = y ⇒ ☐ x = y). (What pairs (x, y) could be counterexamples? Not pairs of distinct objects, for then the antecedent is false; nor any pair of an object and itself, for then the consequent is true.) If 'a' and 'b' are rigid designators, it follows that 'a = b', if true, is a necessary truth. If 'a' and 'b' are not rigid designators, no such conclusion follows about the statement 'a = b' (though the objects designated by 'a' and 'b' will be necessarily identical).

The sense of “Leibnitz's law” that Kripke relies upon here is the less controversial (but less clearly said by Leibniz) indiscernability of identicals (see identity_of_indiscernables#Leibnizs law):

(x)(y)( x = y ⇒. F(x) ⇒ F(y) )
In this case the open formula in place of F(…) is: ☐ ( x = … ) producing:
(x)(y)( x = y ⇒. ☐(x = x) ⇒ ☐(x = y) )

from that and (z)☐(z = z) it may be inferred that

internal relations - all or none ??

At first glance this appears to be a beautiful explication of internal relations. It is at least prima facie appealing that a relation R is internal iff:

(x) (y) ( x R y ⊃ ☐ x R y )

But what gives this the sense of substance is the universal quantification of x and y. Does it really have to be the case that if a relationship is internal for one pair of objects then it is internal for all the pairs of objects for which it obtains? (And likewise for properties, if it is internal for one object/type of object must be be internal for all??). That is the claim made in that Kripke's suggested explication of “internal property”.

However, consider the table in my kitchen and the table in my living room. I presume that it is not necessarily true that my kitchen table is longer than my living room table. If I dub my kitchen table “Alia” and my living room table “Beethoven” so that they are “rigidly designated” by these names then I presume (sadly I seem to lack the relevant modal intuitions) that it is not necessarily the case that Alia is longer than Beethoven. Hence the relationship of being longer than is not an internal relationship by Kripke's definition.

But, to build upon an example Kripke presents in #lecture 1, consider the relationship between 2 metres and 1 metre (if you are uncomfortable reifying meters, me too, but it is Kripke's example). Surely it is necessarily the case that the length rigidly designated (according to Kripke) by “2 meters” is longer than that designated by “1 meter”. Does this make [longer_than] an internal relationship for meter lengths even though it is not internal for every kind of object?

A natural suggestion is to relativise internal relationships to types of object. Relation R is internal for objects of type T iff:

(x)(y) (T(x) ∧ T(y) ∧ x R y .⊃ ☐ x R y)

In this manner we may recognise [longer_than] as an internal relationship for lengths, but not for tables. But this won't seem to do:

weirdness with non-rigid designators

Lets focus on the last sentence:

If 'a' and 'b' are not rigid designators, no such conclusion follows about the statement 'a = b' (though the objects designated by 'a' and 'b' will be necessarily identical).

So lets say that Ali is the only red car on my street. Then “Ali” rigidly designates Ali and the definite description “the red car on my street” non-rigidly designates Ali. So too if Ali is the only Holden on my street then the definite description “the Holden on my street” also non-rigidly designates Ali.

Now it is not true that “Necessarily the red car on my street is the Holden on my street” but it is true that “Necessarily, the object designated by 'the red car on my street' is identical to the object designated by 'the Holden on my street'”. So within the scope of a ☐ replacing a denoting phrase “N” with “the object referred to by the phrase 'N'” does not always preserve truth.

More generally “☐ P(the object referred to by 'Name')” may be true and yet “☐ P(Name)” be false. (We don't have to consider P as just a predicate here, but as any closed formula.) It is, apparently, true by Kripke's standards (given his later claims that objects have their origins essentially) that “Necessarily the object designated by 'the red car on my street' was built in China.” but false that “Necessarily the red car on my street was built in China.”

This does not break the principle that substituting any name for both instances of 'N' in “'N' denotes N” produces a truth. (A principle that seems seems every bit as non-negotiable as “'P' is true iff P”.) But we do have a startling failure of substitutivity that comes close – a strange breach of referential disquotation.

Naively one would have thought that quotation marks around a term (name or definite description) and “the object denoted by by …”, would express kind of inverse operations. Just as quotation marks applied to sentences and “is true”. When one applies “is true” to a quoted sentence one gets another that is true just in case the quoted original is true. Indeed this is what (I thought) the disquotational theory of truth was all about. (See disquotational_truth) But this

This is a high price to pay. For non rigid designators within the scope of necessity operators substitutivity fails. Breaking the complexity of the underlying logic.

If substitutivity fails in this case, if one can't even substitute “the object designated by 'P'” for 'P', without changing truths to falsehoods then there is surely no hope for some criteria for transworld identity (which would amount to a standard for new transworld entities). We could tell when two terms refer to the same entity, and when they do not.

influential_semantic_theories#Kripkes modal argument Phase 1 of this lecture sets about the task of refreshing readers' memory of Fregean/Russellian accounts of (ordinary) names as (analysable into) definite descriptions. (Ordinary names of objects that are not, in Russell's terminology, known by acquaintance.) Then this is widened to include more somewhat more complex (and perhaps more vague, though Kripke doesn't bring that into the picture) then a widening of this account into clusters or families.

At this point Kripke somewhat carelessly mixes two different accounts of names - describing both as “What we really associate with the name is a family of descriptions.” But takes this as describing both

  1. Frege's account that “In the case of genuinely proper names like 'Aristotle' opinions as regards their sense may diverge.” According to which different speakers use the name “Aristotle” to express different senses – but “As long as the nominatum remains the same, these fluctuations in sense are tolerable”. I.e. each competent speaker uses 'Aristotle' in a different sense, but so long as those diverse senses are (uniquely?) true of the same person (i.e. Aristotle) uses each counts as using the name correctly.

AND

  1. The more or less Wittgensteinian view that the name expresses a kind of weighted cluster or family of descriptions. Few or none of them must be true of the object referred to by the relevant name. But a sufficient collection of them is required to be true of the named object (otherwise the named object does not exist/the name fails to refer).

Kripke does justify the shift to (2) on the grounds that “even a single speaker when asked 'What description are you willing to substitute for the name?' may be quite at a loss. In fact, he may know many things about him; but any particular thing that he knows he may feel clearly expresses a contingent property of the object”. But his way of shifting from one to the other may mislead a less than scrupulous reader into confusing the two. He describes them both as ways of explaining how names don't express a simple shared definite description. (But Frege gives up shared, while Wittgenstein gives up simple.)
